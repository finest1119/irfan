## Fact-check

| Claim in the reel | Check | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "Close to four hundred watts" from "around a hundred sixty gallons a minute" and "twenty-six feet of fall" | (a) DOE Energy Saver rule of thumb: net head (ft) × flow (gal/min) ÷ 10 = watts, so 26 × 160 ÷ 10 = 416 W. (b) Physics: 160 gal/min = 10.1 L/s, 26 ft = 7.9 m, 10.1 × 9.81 × 7.9 ≈ 783 W in the water; at 50 % overall efficiency ≈ 390 W. | Two methods agree. **Caveat:** the DOE rule uses NET head (after pipe friction), and 26 ft is the gross fall, so real output would be somewhat lower. "Close to" and "around" keep it honest. |
| DOE rule assumes about 53 % efficiency | Archived DOE Energy Savers page. | Search snippet only; the live energy.gov pages returned 404 to the fetcher in this session. Not spoken or shown. Re-check before posting. |
| "It's packing pressure" / HUD ≈ 11 PSI | 26 ft of water × 0.433 psi/ft ≈ 11.3 psi (static). | Standard hydrostatics. |
| "Runs day and night … around nine kilowatt-hours a day" | 0.39–0.42 kW × 24 h ≈ 9.4–10 kWh/day. | Arithmetic. Assumes steady flow; real creeks drop in dry months. Not claimed as a year-round figure. |

**Changed versus the proposal**
- Numbers kept as in the proposal (160 gal/min, 26 ft, about 400 W, about 9 kWh/day, about a third of a home). I confirmed them with the DOE rule and the EIA 2024 figure.
- VO says "steal a little power … and hand the water right back", which softens the title's "stole" and avoids suggesting the creek is drained.
- Added the second SNAG/HACK pair (pipe friction → fatter pipe). It also answers the net-versus-gross head caveat on screen.
- Torque stays on the dry bank, away from the intake (never in danger).

**Not verified / soft**
- The 53 % efficiency note and the trash-rack source were seen as search snippets only.
- Friction-loss figures are my own estimate and are not used on screen.
- 160 gal/min is a big share of a small creek. The world model should show the creek clearly larger than the diverted flow, to match "leaves plenty of water".

**Safety (already covered by the caption line):** diverting creek water usually needs state water-rights approval and permits (and sometimes federal review); intakes need a fish screen and enough water must stay in the creek. No wiring or plumbing steps are shown as instructions.
