## Voiceover script (9 beats)

### hook
VO: On Friday, November thirteenth, a tanker struck a docked riverboat on the Mississippi River in New Orleans. The riverboat capsized at the dock. Officials said everyone on board was accounted for.
Visual: Bright stylised New Orleans riverfront in midday sun: wide brown-blue river, a white paddlewheel riverboat at a wharf, a long dark tanker gliding upriver in the distance. Card "NEW ORLEANS · 13 NOV 2026". No impact shown.

### question
VO: Investigators say the tanker's steering failed about two minutes earlier, and they are still working out why. So how do big ships end up hitting things on rivers? Here are the three big reasons.
Visual: Top-down toy map of a river with a generic ship icon and a stopwatch chip "ABOUT 2 MINUTES (NTSB, preliminary)"; then title card "HOW DO SHIPS COLLIDE ON RIVERS?".

### one_momentum
VO: One: momentum. A big ship can weigh tens of thousands of tons, and it has no brakes. Even with the engine thrown into reverse, it can travel many times its own length before it stops.
Visual: Side view of a generic cargo ship on bright water; propeller reverses with a froth of bubbles while ghost outlines of the hull stack up ahead of it to show ship lengths travelled.

### two_river
VO: Two: the river itself. The channel is narrow, it bends, and the current never stops pushing. New Orleans has a bend the Coast Guard has called one of the most challenging on the Mississippi.
Visual: Aerial of a stylised crescent-shaped river bend with animated current arrows that grow and shrink; a generic ship crabbing sideways through the turn. Label "ILLUSTRATION".

### three_failure
VO: Three: no room for failure. A rudder only works when water flows past it, and steering needs power. Lose either one at sea and there is space to drift. On a river, the bank, a bridge or a dock can be seconds away.
Visual: Underwater cutaway of a rudder with flow lines bending around it; lines fade and the rudder goes limp. Split screen: same ship on an endless open ocean versus in a narrow river corridor.

### metaphor
VO: It is like steering a loaded truck down an icy alley lined with parked cars.
Visual: Toy truck sliding gently on a glossy ice-blue alley between rows of colourful parked cars; it stops short. Playful, no crash.

### protections
VO: So how is the risk kept low? Local river pilots guide the big ships. A Coast Guard traffic service watches the river around the clock, a bit like air traffic control. And tugs can be called in to help.
Visual: Friendly control room with a big river map and moving ship dots; a pilot boat pulls alongside a ship; two bright tugs nudge a hull.

### takeaway
VO: Takeaway: on a river cruise or a ferry, treat the safety briefing like the one on a plane. Know where the life jackets are, and know your nearest way out.
Visual: Cheerful ferry deck: orange life jackets glowing in a locker, green exit arrows lighting up along the deck.

### outro
VO: Have you ever watched a giant ship squeeze past on a river? Tell us where in the comments, and send this to someone who loves cruises. Because in New Orleans, investigators say it all happened in about two minutes.
Visual: Calm golden-hour riverfront, the tanker silhouette passing safely mid-river, comment and share icons; stopwatch chip "ABOUT 2 MINUTES" returns.
